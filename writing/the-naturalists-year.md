# The Naturalist's Year

### Thirty-Six Field Notes, One Calendar, and the Birds That Kept Me Company

---

## Preface

For forty years I have carried a notebook into the field. The early ones are disappointing — rapid, clumsy lists of names, written in haste, as if the important thing were to record that a bird had been present at all. In recent years I have tried to write differently. I sit longer. I describe the light, the weather, the sound a bird made just before it vanished, and the small, unremarkable people who stand beside me without knowing they are part of the entry.

The thirty-six notes that follow were written across a single calendar year, from a frozen January to the following December. I travelled more than I intended to. Some entries were written in the field, pencil in a stiff hand; others by lamplight in a rented room, days afterwards. A few are reconstructed from memory, which is a poor instrument but the only one I had.

I have not arranged these notes by species or by place. They are in the order I lived them. The birds are the constants: the robin that begins the year singing in the half-light, the warbler that returns to the same patch of desert after a winter hidden in a crevice, the geese that turn a flooded field into an illusion of snow, and the small black-and-white bird that appears in three entries without my ever knowing whether it is the same bird at all.

Read them as a year, not a catalogue. That is how I wrote them.

— *E. V., in the last week of December*

# WINTER

*The year began cold. I had intended to stay indoors and sort the previous year's notes, but the birds had other plans — irruptions from the north, a rare owl on the dunes, a bird asleep in a crevice that I would not have believed if I had not measured its breathing myself. Winter is not an empty season. It is a season of visitors.*

---

## 1. 8 January — The Waxwing Invasion

*Written in the town park, at midday, with cold fingers. I had come to look at the rowan and found the tree occupied by birds I had not seen in three years.*

Peter first noticed them on a grey morning in late January, standing in the rowan tree at the end of the town park: a flock of birds he did not recognize, sleek and cinnamon-crested, with black masks across their eyes and small yellow tips on their wings that looked like drops of wax. They were Bohemian Waxwings, though he did not learn the name until later, and there were perhaps forty of them, passing from berry to berry along the branches in a slow, orderly line. What struck him most was their quietness. They did not chatter or squabble. They fed with a kind of silent concentration, and when one bird moved to a new branch, the next one moved into the space it had left, so that the whole flock seemed to flow along the limb like a single animal.

He came back the next day and they were still there. Over the following week he watched them strip the tree. He learned that waxwings were nomads, appearing in unpredictable irruptions when the berry crops failed in the north, and that a flock could consume an astonishing number of berries in a day. He learned about the waxy red tips on their wing feathers that gave them their name, and their soft, high, trilling call — a sound he came to recognize before he could see them. One afternoon he saw a bird on the pavement beneath the tree that seemed unsteady, stumbling as it walked, and he remembered something he had read about waxwings becoming intoxicated on fermented berries that had been left too long in the warmth. He stood over it for a while, but it recovered and flew up to rejoin the flock, and he never saw another one behave that way.

The tree was bare by the end of the second week. One morning in February he found the flock in a different part of town, stripping the crabapples in a hospital garden, and he stood there with his coffee, watching the same orderly passage along the branches. Then, on a day in early March, they were gone. He looked for them every morning for the rest of the winter, scanning the rowan, the crabapples, the hedgerows along the river, but he never saw them again. He had no idea where they had gone — north, perhaps, or east, or anywhere the berries were. He only knew that for three weeks a small, beautiful, silent flock had passed through his town, and that the tree had been left bare behind them.

## 2. 19 January — The White Ghost

*An irruption year. The rarity drew nineteen cars to a narrow road, and I stood among the birders with my notebook, ashamed of how much I wanted the bird to stay still.*

The snowy owl sat on the dune like a statue that someone had forgotten to take down, and by noon there were nineteen cars parked along the narrow road. It was January, and the owl was a long way from home — an irruption year, when the lemming population in the Arctic had collapsed and the great white birds had flown south in search of food, turning up on beaches, airfields, and dunes where no one expected them. A woman in a fur hat raised her camera; the owl watched her over one yellow eye and did not move.

It was enormous, broad as a turkey, with a round head and no ear tufts, and its plumage was white with dark barring that made it look speckled against the snow — not pure white at all, but the color of old paper. Every few minutes it shifted its weight or flicked a wing, and a ripple went through the crowd of birders, a series of small gasps. At two o'clock a rabbit appeared at the base of the dune, and the owl's head turned with a mechanical slowness, first one eye, then the other fixing on the movement. It launched without a sound. The rabbit saw it at the last instant and bolted for the marram grass; the owl struck, missed, and landed a dozen yards up the beach, where it sat for twenty minutes composing itself while the birders let out the breath they had been holding.

By four the light had gone flat and the cars began to leave. The owl was still on the dune, or another like it, white against the white sand, waiting for the next rabbit, or the next lemming, or for the long flight north when the sun came back to the tundra.

## 3. 27 January — The Sleeper

*I have checked this bird ten times now. Each time I expect to find it dead. Each time the faint movement of its breast is still there.*

In January the biologist found the common poorwill in a crevice between two rocks, and at first she thought it was dead. It was a small, mottled bird, a relative of the nightjars, and it sat with its eyes closed and its feathers fluffed, so still that she nearly put her hand on it before she saw the faint rise and fall of its breast. She knew what it was doing. Poorwills were the only birds known to enter a true, prolonged torpor — a kind of hibernation — surviving the cold desert winter by dropping their body temperature and slowing their breathing to almost nothing. She marked the spot, and came back every ten days with a torch and a thermometer, shining the light gently on the bird, checking that it was still there. Week after week it sat in the same crevice, apparently asleep, living on stored fat.

In April she came and the crevice was empty. She did not know where it had gone, or whether it had survived the winter at all. Then, on a warm evening in May, she was sitting outside her trailer at dusk when she heard it: a soft, repetitive whistle from the slope above the camp, *poor-will, poor-will, poor-will*, each phrase followed by a short pause. It was the bird's call, and it was a male claiming a territory. She got up and walked toward the sound, but it stopped as she approached; she stood in the darkness and waited, and after a few minutes it began again, farther off. She never saw it, but she knew it was the same bird — or one like it, returned to this patch of desert — and she went back inside pleased.

## 4. 3 February — The Dipper

*On the bridge, in sleet. It sang three times while I stood there, as if the river belonged to it — which, in a sense, it does.*

In January the river was cold enough that the stones along its bank were rimed with ice, but the dipper sang as if it were spring. A woman stood on the bridge above the water and watched it on a rock in the middle of the stream: a plump, dark bird with a white throat and a white belly, bobbing up and down incessantly — the dip that gave it its name. Then it walked into the water and disappeared beneath the surface. She knew what it was doing. Dippers were the only songbirds that could hunt underwater, walking along the riverbed with their wings held half-open, gripping the stones with strong feet, picking off aquatic insects and small fish. They had a third eyelid, a nictitating membrane, that they drew across their eyes for protection, and they could stay submerged for half a minute at a time.

The bird surfaced a few yards downstream, shook itself, and returned to its rock, where it sang again — a loud, rich, warbling song that carried over the sound of the water. She had read that dippers sang in winter, defending their stretch of river, and that a pair might hold the same territory for years. Another dipper appeared upstream, and the first bird's song grew more insistent; the two chased each other over the water for a minute, then the intruder flew off and the resident returned to its rock. The woman watched for another ten minutes, but the bird had gone back to hunting, and she walked on across the bridge with its song still in her ears.

## 5. 14 February — The Crossbills

*The forester had been noting their arrival for eight years. He keeps a notebook too, though he would not call it that.*

The crossbills arrived in the pine plantation in the third week of February, and the forester noticed them because of the noise: a continual, sharp *kip-kip-kip* from the tops of the trees, and the sound of cones being torn apart. He walked out between the rows of pines and found them — a flock of perhaps thirty birds, moderately large, the males a dull brick-red and the females olive-grey, all with stout bills whose tips crossed over each other like a pair of pliers. That crossed bill was the whole secret of the bird: it was a tool for prising open pine cones, which the crossbills did with astonishing speed, wedging the bill between the scales and twisting, then extracting the seed with their tongues.

He stood beneath a tree and watched them work. They hung upside down from the cones, clung to the branches, shuffled along the twigs, always with that busy, mechanical *kip*. They were nomads, appearing in winter when the cone crop in the north failed, and they could be surprisingly tame; one male, red as a brick, allowed him to come within ten feet before it flew to the next tree. On the ground beneath the pines, the discarded cone scales lay in piles, and he could see the fresh, pale wood where the birds had been at work.

The flock stayed four days. Then, one morning in early March, the plantation was quiet, and the forester found only the empty husks beneath the trees. He wrote the date in his notebook, as he did every year, and thought about where the birds had gone — north, perhaps, to the boreal forests, or east, or wherever the cones were opening. He never knew. He only knew that for four days the pines had been full of them, and that the work they did — twisting the cones open, one seed at a time — was something no other bird in the wood could manage.

## 6. 22 February — The Storm Birds

*I cannot decide whether the fulmars enjoy the storm or merely tolerate it. Watching them ride the gale, I lean toward enjoyment.*

The gale hit the coast at noon, and by afternoon the sea was grey and the rain came horizontal. A man named David, walking the cliff path in a proper storm coat, stopped to watch the fulmars. They were riding the wind as if it were a solid thing — long-winged, grey-white, with tube-shaped nostrils and a shearing flight that seemed to need no effort at all. They skimmed the cliff face, turned on the wind, swept out over the churning water and back, never flapping, apparently enjoying themselves. Other birds had gone to ground; the fulmars owned the storm.

He had read that fulmars were among the most pelagic of gull-like birds, spending most of their lives at sea and coming to cliffs only to breed, and that they were built for exactly this — stiff wings, a streamlined body, and the ability to store oil in their stomachs, which they could spit at predators with surprising accuracy. One of the birds on a nearby ledge faced a rival, opened its mouth, and squirted a thin stream of amber oil; the other bird stepped back, and the first settled onto its nest. Through the spray David could see the nest tucked into a crevice, and the dark shape of a chick huddled there. The parents took turns incubating, he knew, and both would stay with the chick through the roughest weather. He stood in the rain until his hood was soaked through, watching them ride the gale, then turned back along the path with the wind at his back.

## 7. 1 March — The Bait

*The mangroves at low tide. I thought I had come for the spoonbills; I stayed for the heron with the leaf.*

The boat slid silently through the mangroves at low tide, and the bird life was everywhere. Roseate spoonbills waded in the shallows, swinging their flat, spatulate bills from side to side through the water, filtering out small fish and crustaceans; white ibises probed the mud with their long, curved bills; a great egret stood motionless at the edge of a pool, waiting. A woman in the boat with a camera watched them all, but it was a small green heron on a root nearby that held her attention.

The heron had picked up a small leaf and dropped it on the surface of the water. It watched as the leaf drifted, and when a fish rose to investigate, the heron struck — quick as a flick of the wrist — and caught the fish in its bill. She had read that green herons were among the few birds known to use tools, and that some had been observed using bread crusts, insects, or leaves as bait to lure fish within striking range. This one repeated the performance three times, each time with a fresh leaf, and each time it seemed to work. A green heron, she thought, was an angler.

An alligator lay half-submerged nearby, and the birds kept a respectful distance from it, though the spoonbills waded within a few feet, apparently unafraid. As the tide began to rise the wading birds drifted toward the trees, and the boat turned back toward the channel. The woman lowered her camera. The mangroves were quiet again, except for the gentle lap of water among the roots and the occasional splash of a feeding bird.

# SPRING

*I moved north as the weather softened. Spring is the loudest season: rooks in the beeches, grouse booming on the prairie, a mockingbird in a suburban hydrangea singing through the small hours because a porch light has told him it is day. And everywhere, the glass of buildings, reflecting the sky and taking birds out of it.*

## 8. 12 March — The Drummer

*I found the bird by listening for it, not by looking. A downy woodpecker can make a gutter sound like a whole flock of birds.*

Daniel moved into the house in the first week of March and was woken on his third morning by a sound like someone rattling a loose coin in a tin can — fast, dry, and coming from outside his bedroom window. He went to the kitchen, made coffee, and pulled the curtain. On the metal gutter two feet from the glass clung a small black-and-white bird, hammering its head against the channel in short bursts, fifteen or twenty blows at a time, then stopping to look around.

It was a downy woodpecker, and it was not eating. There were no insects in the gutter. It was drumming — claiming the street as its territory before the females arrived, choosing the hollow metal because the sound carried farther than any branch could. Daniel banged on the window; the bird stopped, flattened itself against the gutter, and flew to the maple. Five minutes later it was back.

He looked it up over coffee and learned that the drumming was harmless, that it would not damage the gutter, and that it would stop when the bird found a mate. He draped an old towel over that stretch of channel anyway. The first tap was dull and went nowhere; the woodpecker tried twice more, seemed to consider the problem, and flew off to the house next door, where the gutters were still bright and resonant.

Daniel slept through the following night. But by the second week of April the drumming had moved back down the street, softer now, interspersed with a low, rapid chatter he did not recognize. He found the bird one morning in the maple with a second woodpecker following close behind it, both flicking through the new leaves, the male pausing now and then to tap a branch — not the loud territorial roll, but a quiet, private sound between the two of them.

## 9. 28 March — The Rookery

*Two hundred nests in the tops of the beeches, and a hundred birds arriving and leaving at once. The sound carries across the fields.*

The rookery stood in the tall beeches at the edge of the village, and you could hear it before you could see it — a great, cawing clamor that carried across the fields on the March wind. From the lane below, a woman named Margaret watched the rooks come and go. There must have been two hundred nests in the crowns of the trees, each a rough cup of twigs, and the birds were rebuilding them after a winter of damage, hauling sticks in from the surrounding ploughed fields.

It was not a peaceful place. A rook would land at a nest with a twig in its bill and immediately another rook would arrive, bowing and cawing, and attempt to pull the stick out; sometimes the thief succeeded and flew off with it, and sometimes there was a brief, flapping struggle that ended with both birds in the next tree. Margaret knew from reading that rookeries were organized by strict hierarchies — the biggest, strongest males held the best sites near the center, while the younger birds made do at the edges — and that twig-stealing was a normal part of the business, since a good nest was worth fighting for. She watched one bird with a damaged leg hop awkwardly along a branch, draped in the discarded twigs of its neighbors, and wondered how it managed at all.

Lower down, a pair of rooks were engaged in a slow, deliberate bowing display, dipping their heads, raising their tail feathers, calling softly to each other — the pair bond being renewed after the winter. Above them the great clamor continued, the cawing rising and falling in waves as birds arrived or departed. Then, as the light began to go, a hundred rooks rose from the beeches at once and wheeled away toward the roosting woods, and the trees were suddenly still, with only the dark shapes of the nests left against the sky.

## 10. 5 April — The White Tide

*April, and a field that looked like snow. I have seen this three times and it still stops me in my tracks.*

Eleanor had lived beside the flooded cornfield for thirty-one years and had never once seen it snow in April, which was why she stood at the kitchen window with her coffee going cold and watched the field turn white. The geese arrived in waves. They came out of the grey sky in long, wavering lines, white as torn paper, and poured down onto the water until the whole field seemed to be covered in snowdrifts that shifted and flowed and honked. They were Snow Geese — greater snow geese, on their way north to the Arctic, stopping here to feed in the shallow water — and there must have been ten thousand of them.

The noise was the first thing. It was not a chorus so much as a continuous, nasal clamor, a thousand small horns blown badly and all at once, rising and falling as the flock shifted. Mixed through it was the sound of their feeding: a wet, industrious tearing as they grubbed in the soft mud for leftover corn and the roots of marsh plants, their orange bills stripping the field bare. The smell came through the window too — wet earth, and the sharp, grassy reek of goose droppings. Eleanor had never minded it. It meant the migration was on schedule.

She had learned to read the flock. At the edges stood a few birds with their necks stretched high, watching, while the rest fed with their heads down and tails cocked. Every so often one of the sentries gave a call and a ripple of movement passed through the whole white mass, as if the field itself had shivered — then settled again. A pair of goslings would not appear for weeks, up on the nesting grounds; these birds were still in their winter plumage, heading north to breed, and they were eating here to build the fat they would need for the last leg and then for the nesting season. She knew all this because she had looked it up, year after year, each spring when they came.

By seven o'clock the field was stripped. The geese began to lift in sections, the water exploding into white wings, and then the whole flock was rising at once — a white tide pulling off the land, twisting in the light, wheeling once above the trees before it streamed north and thinned to nothing against the sky. The silence afterward was enormous. Eleanor finished her coffee at the sink. She thought of the tundra, the nesting grounds, the long way still to go. Then she put on her boots and went out to check the drainage ditch, because the water had to come down before the planting.

## 11. 17 April — The Quok at Dusk

*Night-herons in the willows by the reservoir. They wait for the light to go before they begin — the night is their day.*

The heronry was in a stand of tall willows at the edge of the reservoir, and the birds did not appear until the light had nearly gone. Alan had been coming to the hides at dusk for a fortnight, and he had learned their schedule. The black-crowned night-herons left the trees about twenty minutes after sunset, one or two at a time at first, then in a steady stream, their stocky grey bodies and short necks unmistakable even in the failing light. They flew low over the water, giving a harsh, barking call — *quok* — that sounded like a dog yapping far away.

He knew that these were among the few herons that hunted at night, and that their red eyes were adapted for the dark; that they nested colonially, often in trees beside water, and that a colony could be noisy enough to hear from half a mile away. Tonight, as the last of the birds cleared the willows, the noise from the trees did not stop — there were still young in the nests, calling to be fed, and adults that had not yet departed. A great horned owl called from the far bank, once, and the herons' clamor dipped, then rose again.

Alan sat in the hide until it was fully dark, listening to the *quok* calls coming from the water below, where the herons were hunting the shallows. He could not see them, only hear the occasional splash and the harsh cries as they claimed fishing spots. When he finally walked back to his car, the willows were silent, and the only sound was the wind in the leaves. He came back the following evening, and the evening after that, and always the same thing: the birds waited for the light to go before they began. The night was their day.

## 12. 29 April — The Booming Ground

*Before sunrise on the prairie. The sound carries a mile across the grass — a low, ventriloqual booming that seems to come from the ground itself.*

Before dawn in the third week of April, a man named Ray sat in a blind at the edge of a prairie and waited for the grouse to arrive. The lek — the display ground — was a bare patch of ground among the grasses, and for weeks the males had been coming to it before sunrise. As the sky lightened they appeared from the surrounding brush, one by one: plump, mottled birds with air sacs on their necks that they inflated until they were the size of oranges. Then the booming began. It was a deep, resonant sound that carried across the prairie — *boom-boom-boom* — made by the air sacs, and with each boom the bird stamped its feet and lowered its head, its tail spread like a fan.

There were perhaps twenty males on the lek, each with a small territory a few yards across, and the displaying went on continuously for two hours. The stronger birds held the center; the younger ones were pushed to the edges, and occasionally two males met in a brief, flapping fight that ended as quickly as it began. Ray had read that the females — plain brown hens — came to the lek only to choose a mate, and that a single male might do most of the mating. Sure enough, as the sun came up, three hens walked in from the grass and stood at the edge of the display ground, apparently indifferent, watching the males boom and stomp and shuffle. One of them eventually walked to the center bird, and he mounted her; then she left, and the males resumed their displaying as if nothing had happened.

By seven o'clock the booming had stopped and the birds had dispersed into the prairie. Ray walked back to his car, his ears still ringing with the sound. He had heard it from a mile away that morning, while it was still dark.

## 13. 4 May — The All-Night Singer

*Written at dawn, after a night with very little sleep. A mockingbird under a streetlamp will sing until someone turns the light off.*

Nina had been awake since midnight with a fever, and by three o'clock she had begun to notice the mockingbird. It sat in the hydrangea beneath her bedroom window, outlined by the porch light that her father always left on, and it sang as if the hour were noon. The song was not one song but a whole catalog: the shriek of a jay, the cheep of a sparrow, the creak of a rusty gate, the whistle of a boy calling a dog — all of it delivered in the same harsh, scratchy voice, repeated in long, untiring phrases. She had read that mockers could imitate dozens of other birds, and sometimes mechanical sounds, and that the repertoire grew with age. This one sounded like an old male with a great deal to say.

She lay on her back and timed the pauses. He would sing for perhaps forty seconds, then stop and listen, head tilted, for five or ten; then begin again, sometimes with the same phrase, sometimes with a new one. Once he flew down to the lawn and chased a cat that had wandered too near the hydrangea, uttering a sharp, scolding note; then he was back on his branch and singing again. At four she got up and went to the window. The light from the porch washed the garden in a hard yellow pool, and the bird sat at the center of it, his breast a pale streak, absolutely unafraid of her. She had read that mockingbirds sometimes sang through the night when artificial light confused their internal clocks — that a bird tricked into thinking it was daytime would keep singing long after a normal bird had gone silent.

She went downstairs, barefoot, and found the switch by the door. She hesitated with her hand on it, thinking of the bird on his branch in the middle of his song. Then she turned off the porch light. The garden went black. From outside came one last phrase, cut off in the middle, and then nothing. The silence was so complete that she stood there listening for a full minute, certain she had done something cruel. She went back up to bed and slept until nine.

In the morning the hydrangea was empty. At noon she looked again and saw him on the fence at the end of the garden, preening in ordinary daylight, giving an occasional short phrase as if nothing unusual had happened. She left the light off that night, and the next, and the night after that. The house was dark; the garden was quiet; she slept. She never saw him singing in the dark again, but sometimes, waking at three, she found herself listening for the sound, and felt a small, unreasonable regret at the absence of it.

## 14. 11 May — The Glass Wall

*The building manager allows me up at dawn. I have stood on that floor in four different springs and hoped each time for an empty one.*

Ray had worked the night shift at the atrium for eleven years and had dreaded dawn every spring since the first one. At five-thirty in May he found them scattered across the polished floor beneath the great glass wall: a thrush, two warblers, a vireo, a small green heron that must have flown up the river and mistaken the building for the sky. Some were already dead. Others lay on their backs with their feet in the air, breathing fast — stunned, not broken, which meant there was still time if he moved quickly.

He had learned what was happening from a woman at the wildlife center. The glass reflected the sky and the trees, and migrating birds could not tell reflection from reality. At night and at dawn the lights inside the building drew them in, and they flew full speed into a wall that looked like open air. The museum across town had the same problem every year; so did the airport, and the tall hotel on the waterfront. It was not the height that killed them, she said, but the light. Turn the lights off and the birds passed right by.

Ray kept a supply of cardboard boxes in the security office. He scooped the stunned birds up gently, placed each in a dark box with a soft cloth, and set the boxes in a quiet, warm corner. Disorientation passed in an hour or two; then he took them outside, opened the boxes, and watched them fly off into the dark. The dead ones he bagged and labeled for the center. On a bad morning he found thirty. On the worst morning, three years ago, he had counted sixty-one, including a bittern that should have been in a marsh a hundred miles away.

He wrote his first memo in his second year. Management thanked him and did nothing. He wrote another, and another, and in the fourth year he attached a photograph of the floor at dawn — the small bodies lying there like fallen leaves — and a printout of what the center had told him about lights and migration. There was a meeting. There were objections: the cost, the appearance of a dark building, the inconvenience to the cleaning crews. Ray said nothing about the birds; he only kept putting the photographs in front of people. In the sixth year the building agreed to a trial: during the spring and fall migration windows, the atrium lights would go out between midnight and six.

The next fall the floor was almost clean. A few birds still struck the lower panes, where the reflection of a planter misled them, but the toll fell by more than half. Ray stopped dreading dawn. Some mornings now he walked the floor with his coffee and found nothing at all, and he would stand for a minute at the glass, looking out at the sky the birds saw, thinking of the ones that had passed over in the dark and kept going. Then he went back to his desk and finished his shift.

## 15. 19 May — The First Shift

*I caught the city at five, before the buses. The birds have the square to themselves for perhaps an hour.*

At five in the morning the square belonged to the birds. Pigeons strutted across the wet pavement and clustered on the shoulders of the statue, cooing softly; a pair of ring-necked parakeets chattered in the plane trees, bright green in the lamplight; and from the roof of the bank a blackbird sang a clear, fluting phrase, over and over, into the empty streets. A street cleaner named Hassan pushed his cart along the edge of the square and stopped to listen. He had worked this shift for six years, and he knew the birds by their routines: the blackbird always started first, about twenty minutes before sunrise; the parakeets woke later and noisier; the pigeons were already there, as if they had never left.

He had read that city birds often sang earlier and louder than their country cousins, because they had to compete with the traffic; by seven o'clock the square would be full of buses and the blackbird's song would be lost. He watched a pigeon land on the railing beside him and look up at him with its small, red-eyed face, and he murmured something to it in Arabic; it did not move. A woman waiting for a bus stood near the plane trees and threw a crust of bread, and the pigeons descended in a flurry, and the parakeets scolded from above. Then the first bus came around the corner, and the blackbird's song was swallowed by the sound of the engine, and the square filled with people. The birds went on with their business, as they always did, through the noise.

## 16. 30 May — The Peregrines

*Twenty floors up, on a ledge, in a wooden box. Four chicks this year. The building manager keeps a camera on them and has shown me the recordings.*

On the twentieth floor of an office block, a building manager named Tony kept a small video camera trained on a wooden box mounted on a ledge. Inside the box, a peregrine falcon sat on four eggs. He had installed the box five years earlier, after a pair of peregrines had begun nesting on the building, and he had watched them raise chick after chick ever since. The female spent most of her time in the box, turning the eggs, while the male hunted over the city — Tony sometimes saw him stoop from a great height, folding his wings into a dive that could reach two hundred miles an hour, and strike a pigeon in mid-air. The city's pigeons were the falcons' main food supply, which was why the pair had chosen the skyscraper in the first place.

In May the eggs hatched. Tony watched on his monitor as the female brooded four tiny, white chicks, and he saw the male arrive with food — a pigeon, plucked and torn — and hand it to her at the edge of the ledge. By June the chicks were nearly as big as their parents and were exercising their wings on the ledge, jumping and flapping, and in the third week of the month the first one launched itself into the air. Tony was on the roof when it happened. The young falcon stepped off the ledge, dropped a few feet, then caught the air with its long wings and circled the building once, calling loudly, before landing on a nearby parapet. The female called back. Tony watched until all four had flown, then closed the box for another year.

## 17. 3 June — The Plunge

*I sat on a cliff path until the light failed. The noise of the colony is constant, but the diving has a rhythm of its own.*

Helen had come to the cliff path in the afternoon of a windy June day, and the gannets were already there — hundreds of them, wheeling over the water below in a loose, endless circle, white wings black-tipped against the grey sea. She had never seen a gannet colony before. The noise carried all the way up: a continuous, raucous clamor from the nesting ledges, where the birds stood shoulder to shoulder on their nests of seaweed and guano, guarding chicks that were nearly as big as they were. The smell came up too, sharp and fishy, and she had been warned about it by the woman at the visitor center, who had been right.

She found a place to sit on the path and raised her camera. Below her, one bird broke out of the circle, folded its wings completely, and dropped. It fell straight down — forty meters, perhaps more — with a speed that seemed impossible for something so large, and struck the water with a smack that she heard clearly above the wind. A ring of white foam spread out. When the gannet surfaced, a silver fish hung crosswise in its bill; it tossed its head once and the fish disappeared. Then it flapped up again, dripping, and rejoined the circle as if it had done nothing remarkable.

It was the speed that held her. The bird had gone from a glide to a dive in a single movement, and the impact could have stunned it — gannets sometimes hit the water so hard that they are knocked senseless and drowned, or taken by a waiting shark — but this one had risen at once. She took photograph after photograph, and each time she managed to catch a bird in the clean, folded drop, the whole body reduced to a white arrow. She began to see the pattern of the colony: they did not dive at random but followed one another in a slow, rolling succession, so that the sea below the cliffs was continually speckled with falling birds and rising rings of foam.

On a ledge below her, an adult returned to its nest and was met by a chick half again its size, screaming and beating its wings. The adult opened its bill and the chick thrust its own inside, taking the regurgitated fish in one convulsive movement. Helen lowered the camera and watched. The chick's down was still pale; in a few weeks it would fledge, launch itself from the ledge, and begin the same plunge. She stayed until the light went flat and the wind got cold, and on the boat back to the mainland she sat quietly with the camera on her knee, still seeing in her mind that white arrow, the folded wings, the strike.

## 18. 9 June — Eyeshine on the Road

*Two red sparks in the headlights at two in the morning. I have thought about that bird often since — how close it came, and how little it seemed to know about cars.*

Claire came over the ridge at two in the morning, tired enough that the road seemed to shimmer ahead of her, and caught two bright red sparks in the headlights, low at the left-hand verge. She braked hard. The sparks did not move, and as her eyes adjusted she made out the shape of a bird sitting on the warm tarmac — flat to the road, wings held close, utterly still. She had read somewhere that nightjars did this, settling on country roads after dark because the asphalt held the day's heat, and that drivers rarely saw them until it was too late. She stopped the car and left the engine running.

Through the windscreen she studied it. It was a European nightjar, mottled brown and grey, patterned like dead leaves and bark, and at rest it looked like nothing so much as a piece of the road itself. The red glow was the tapetum in its eyes reflecting her headlights — the same effect that made a dog's eyes shine, only brighter. Its bill was tiny, but its mouth, when it opened, was enormous, fringed with stiff bristles that helped scoop insects out of the air. The old name for the bird was goatsucker, from a medieval belief that it milked goats at night; the truth was stranger and more ordinary — it hunted moths.

She switched off the headlights and waited in the dark. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the bird rose straight up without a sound — nightjars fly silently, like owls — and she saw its mouth open wide as it snapped something out of the air, a pale moth no bigger than a thumbnail. It turned once over the hedge and was gone into the darkness, and the red sparks vanished with it. Claire sat for a while with her hands on the wheel. It had been a near thing; a little faster, a little less awake, and she would have felt the bump and never known what she had hit.

She drove the rest of the way home at forty miles an hour, well under the limit, watching the verges. Near the village she saw another pair of red sparks, farther off this time, and slowed again — but it was only a reflection in a wet signpost. At home she parked, went inside, and made herself tea. She thought about the bird's one brief, silent ascent, the mouth opening, the moth disappearing. She had no idea whether it lived a long life or a short one, whether it would reach Africa that autumn or fall to a fox or a cat or another car. But it had flown up out of her headlights and gone about its business, and she had let it. That, at two in the morning, felt like enough.

## 19. 14 June — The Burrow

*The smell of a puffin colony arrives before the birds do. Fish and guano, and the low purr of chicks underground.*

The boat put them ashore below the cliffs at the height of a grey June day, and the smell arrived before the birds did: a sharp, fishy, ammonia reek that seemed to cling to the back of the throat. The clifftop was white with them. Thousands of puffins stood in ranks on the grass and the rock ledges, black backs and white bellies, their great triangular bills stacked with silver sand eels — eleven, twelve at a time, held crosswise so that the fish hung from either side like a moustache. They chattered continuously, a low, guttural babble that rose and fell across the whole colony.

A visitor with a camera sat down at the edge and watched one bird come in from the sea, circle twice, and land awkwardly among the others, immediately turning its bill sideways so the sand eels fanned out. A herring gull made a pass at it; the puffin snapped its bill shut, lost two fish, and vanished into a burrow in the turf. From underground came a strange sound — a low, purring growl, like a small engine — the chick demanding food. Minutes later the adult emerged, beak now empty, and launched itself back over the cliff.

Great skuas patrolled the far end of the colony, long-winged and pirate-minded, and the air filled with the trilling of the puffins whenever one passed overhead. The visitors stayed until the boat's horn sounded from below. All around them, in the flat midday light, the business of the colony continued without pause: fish delivered, chicks fed, burrows guarded, the grass worn bare around every entrance. At sea beyond the cliff, hundreds more puffins floated in rafts, waiting their turn.

## 20. 18 June — The Fig Tree

*Before dawn in the rainforest, and the fig is already full of birds. I filled a notebook and then simply watched.*

At five-thirty the rainforest was still black, and the howler monkeys had already started. Then, one by one, the birds began: a tinamou's hollow whistle from the floor of the forest, the rattling call of a pair of cracids, the thin, high notes of a flock of tanagers moving through the canopy. By six the whole forest was alive with sound, and at the edge of the clearing a great fig tree, heavy with fruit, had become the center of it.

Robert had walked out before dawn to sit beneath it, and now he wrote rapidly as the birds arrived in waves. First came the keel-billed toucans, absurd and beautiful, their enormous orange-and-green bills reaching into the branches to pluck figs which they tossed back and swallowed whole. A flock of common tanagers followed, small and active, picking at the smaller fruit; then a pair of honeycreepers, iridescent green, probing the blossoms with curved bills. A hummingbird held station at a cluster of red flowers, a tiny ruler of its own small patch of air, driving off another of its kind with a high, thin chatter.

Above them all, a pair of scarlet macaws flew over the clearing, squawking, their long tails streaming behind them — a flash of pure red and blue against the grey light. The toucans ignored them. A fig fell with a soft thud nearby and a squirrel took it, bounding up the trunk. Robert wrote until the notebook was full, then sat back and watched. The dawn chorus was winding down now; the specialized calls were being replaced by the ordinary busy sounds of feeding. He had read that a single fruiting fig could support more species of birds than any other tree in the forest, and sitting there, with figs raining softly into the leaves around him, he believed it.

## 21. 25 June — The Wash

*Midday in the desert, and even the birds have stopped moving. Everything is a matter of shade.*

The desert wash had been dry for months, and in the June heat even the birds seemed to move slowly. A Gambel's quail family — a male with a teardrop plume on his head, a hen, and six tiny chicks — scratched in the litter beneath a mesquite tree, half-hidden in the shade. The chicks were no bigger than walnuts, striped like little machines, and they followed their mother so closely that she appeared to be a single bird with six trailing feet. They pecked at seeds and at the small insects stirred up by the scratch of the adults' feet.

A Gila woodpecker hammered at a saguaro cactus nearby, working on a hole it had hollowed out the previous year; the nest would be lined with the fibers of a cactus fruit and used again. The sound of the drilling carried across the wash, dry and regular. Then a greater roadrunner appeared, running hard along the sandy bed — long legs, long tail, crest raised — and the quail family froze and vanished into the brush. The roadrunner skidded, turned, and snatched a lizard from a rock with its heavy bill, beat it once against the ground, and swallowed it head first. It ran on, and the quail emerged cautiously, one chick at a time, as if counting.

High above, a red-tailed hawk circled in the shimmering air, riding a thermal that had built up from the baked ground. The quail pressed flat into the mesquite shade; the woodpecker stopped drilling and flattened itself against the cactus. The hawk drifted away south, and the wash resumed its small, patient business. In the desert at midday, survival was mostly a matter of knowing when to stop moving.

## 22. 30 June — The Bee-Eaters

*A sandy bank above the river, honeycombed with nests. The birds are almost too beautiful to be real, and then one of them rubs a wasp against a branch.*

The sandy bank above the river was honeycombed with holes, and in June it was alive with bee-eaters. They were startlingly beautiful — green backs, blue throats, chestnut heads, black eye-stripes, long curved tails — and there were perhaps fifty pairs nesting in the vertical face of the bank, each in a tunnel drilled into the sand. The birds perched on the branches above, calling softly, or sallied out over the water in pursuit of insects, their wings flashing in the sun.

A woman on the opposite bank watched them through binoculars. She had read that bee-eaters caught bees and wasps in the air, and that they had a peculiar way of preparing their prey: they caught the insect in their bills, returned to a perch, and rubbed the abdomen against the branch until the sting and most of the venom were squeezed out, then tossed the insect up and swallowed it whole. She saw one do this with a large yellow jacket — a long, careful rubbing, a toss, a snap of the bill — and she shivered in sympathy. The bird seemed unharmed.

At the nesting bank, a pair was taking turns at the entrance of one of the tunnels, disappearing inside and emerging covered in sand dust. The holes were shallow at first, then deepened into a chamber where the eggs would be laid; both birds helped dig. Below them, a pair of kingfishers — a flash of orange and blue — shot along the river, and the bee-eaters paid them no attention. Above, a honey buzzard circled, and the whole colony gave a sudden, sharp chorus of calls, the birds rising together from the branches and wheeling above the bank before settling again. The woman stayed until the light went gold and the insects grew scarce, then walked back to the village with the sound of their soft, rolling calls still in her ears.

## 23. 6 July — The Flightless Hour

*July, and the drake is in disguise. I nearly failed to recognize the bird I had watched all spring.*

In July the drake mallard was the ugliest bird on the lake. He had lost his green head and his chestnut breast; he was mottled brown now, patterned like his mate, and he could not fly. Like all ducks, he shed his flight feathers all at once after breeding and spent three weeks grounded while new ones grew — a flightless, defenseless interval that the ducks survived by hiding. He spent most of his days in the reeds at the far end of the water, swimming low, surfacing only his head and the brown line of his back.

A family in a boat drifted past the reed bed one afternoon. The man raised his binoculars and found the drake sitting motionless twenty feet away, watching them with a bright yellow eye. When the boat came closer the duck did not take off — he could not — but slid quietly into the water and swam deeper into the reeds, leaving only a faint V in the surface to show where he had been. A moorhen scolded from the bank; a swallow skimmed the water; the drake vanished completely into the green wall of the reed bed.

By the end of August his flight feathers had grown back. The green returned to his head, the white neck ring, the chestnut breast — the whole splendid disguise reassembled. One evening in September a woman on the shore saw a mallard rise suddenly from the reeds with a roar of wings, circle the lake twice, and settle far out in the open water, a drake again, ready for the autumn flights. She did not know it was the same bird she had seen skulking in July. He knew, and he flew the better for it.

## 24. 12 July — The Clicking Dark

*A cave in the tropics, pitch black, and the sound of thousands of birds seeing by sound. I have never forgotten it.*

The cave on the island was dark enough that the visitors had to use torches, and the sound that came out of it was not what they expected. It was a dry, rapid clicking, thousands of tiny ticks overlapping into a continuous crackle, like rain on a tin roof — and it came from the birds. Edible-nest swiftlets, small grey birds with narrow wings, lived in the cave in their tens of thousands, and because the interior was pitch black they navigated by echolocation, emitting short bursts of sound and listening for the echoes bouncing off the walls. The click was not a call to each other but a kind of seeing.

The visitors stood at the entrance while their guide shone the torch along the rock face. Dotted across the walls and ceiling were the nests — small, crescent-shaped cups made almost entirely of the birds' own saliva, which hardened into a translucent, horn-like material when it dried. These were the nests harvested for bird's nest soup, a delicacy that had made the caves valuable and the swiftlets vulnerable. The guide explained that the birds built them over several weeks, gluing strand after strand of saliva to the rock, and that a pair would return to the same site year after year. Higher up, in the deeper dark, other nests clung to the ceiling, invisible without the torch.

At dusk the swiftlets streamed out of the cave to feed, and the clicking faded as the birds went; a few remained, secreted in the shadows, and here and there a bat emerged. The visitors watched the stream pour out into the twilight — thousands of narrow wings — and thought of the dark cave behind them, where the birds could not see at all but knew exactly where every nest and every wall was, by sound alone.

## 25. 21 July — The Bone Breaker

*In the mountains, a vulture dropped a bone from fifty meters and broke it on the rock. The only bird that does this.*

The hiker saw the bird against the sky above the rocky slope, and at first he thought it was carrying a stick. It was a bearded vulture — a lammergeier — enormous, dark, with long pointed wings and a wedge-shaped tail, and the thing in its talons was a bone, white and long, perhaps the femur of a sheep. He stopped and watched through his binoculars as the bird climbed to perhaps fifty meters above the slope, held the bone in its feet, and then released it. The bone fell, struck a flat rock with a sharp crack, and broke into pieces. The vulture circled down, landed beside the fragments, and began to eat them.

He knew why it did this. The bearded vulture was the only bird — almost the only vertebrate — that deliberately dropped bones from a height to shatter them. Its diet was made up largely of bone marrow, which it could digest because of extraordinarily acidic stomach juices; the marrow of a large bone was a rich meal, and breaking the bone was the only way to get at it. He had read of birds that dropped the same bone three or four times before it broke, and of a famous observation in the Alps where a vulture dropped a tortoise repeatedly until the shell split. This one had been luckier.

A raven landed nearby to see what it could steal, and the vulture hissed and spread its wings; the raven hopped back a pace, but did not leave. The hiker watched for ten minutes as the great bird worked through the fragments, swallowing splinters of bone, then took off heavily and flew away down the valley. He continued up the path. The pieces of bone lay bright on the rock behind him, and the sky above the ridge was empty.

## 26. 2 August — The Defender of the Feeder

*A hummingbird no bigger than a thumb, and he owns the whole garden. I have watched the same bird for six weeks.*

By the second week of August the heat had driven most of the birds out of the garden, but the hummingbird at the feeder stayed. Miriam had hung the feeder in the shade of the porch in June, a simple bottle of sugar water with a red base, and by July a male ruby-throated hummingbird had claimed it. He was a tiny thing, no bigger than a thumb, but he patrolled the garden like a tyrant. His gorget — the patch of feathers at his throat — was ruby red, though from most angles it looked black; only when the sun caught it from exactly the right direction did it flare into color, a jewel suddenly lit. His wings beat so fast they were a grey blur, and he could hang motionless in the air in front of the feeder, his long bill inserted into one of the ports, drinking.

He was not alone for long. Another male appeared in the third week of July, and the battles began. They were brief and violent: a flash of colour, a high, thin chatter, the two birds rising straight up like thrown darts, locking together for a second in mid-air, then separating and chasing each other out over the lawn and into the hedge. The resident always won. He would return to the feeder, perch on a wire above it, click his bill once or twice in what looked like satisfaction, then resume drinking. Miriam sat on the bench with her book and watched these duels, and began to tell the hours by them: the first challenge around eight, another in the heat of the afternoon, a final one before the light went.

Once, a blue jay landed on the railing beside the feeder — a bird twenty times the hummingbird's size. The hummingbird did not hesitate. He shot at it, straight at the jay's face, and the jay, startled, flapped away into the next garden. Miriam laughed out loud. The hummingbird returned to his wire, clicked his bill, and preened, apparently pleased with himself. She had read that ruby-throated hummingbirds migrated south in late summer, crossing the Gulf of Mexico in a single flight, and she watched the bird through August with a new awareness. One morning in the first week of September he was not at the feeder. She checked the wire, the flowers, the hedge. Nothing. She left the feeder up for another week, in case he was late, then took it down. Somewhere, she thought, he was already over the water, flying south through the night.

## 27. 16 August — The Loon's Call

*Two in the morning, and a voice on the lake that sounds almost human. I have never got used to it.*

Tom woke at two with the certain feeling that someone was crying on the lake. He lay in his sleeping bag for a minute, listening. Then it came again — a long, wavering note that slid upward and broke at the end, floating across the water from somewhere out in the dark. He had heard loons before, every summer of his life, but he had never once got used to the sound. There was something human in it, something lonely and deliberate. He unzipped the tent and stepped out onto the shore in his socks, shivering.

The lake was glass-smooth and black, with a few stars reflected in it. The call came again, and this time another voice answered from the far side — a second loon, its note lower, though the distance made it hard to tell. The two voices crossed the water and met somewhere in the middle, overlapping and sliding apart. Tom knew the calls by name now that he thought about it: the long wail was the contact call, used to keep in touch; the laughing tremolo was an alarm; and the yodel, which he had heard once on a different lake, belonged to the male declaring his territory. He stood barefoot on the cold sand until the calling stopped, then went back into the tent and slept.

In the morning he paddled out in the canoe, and there they were, not a hundred yards from camp: a pair of loons with two chicks. The chicks were tiny, striped and downy, and they rode on their mother's back, one on either side, their heads poking up above her wings. When the canoe came too close the female gave the tremolo — the laughing call — and swam away with the chicks huddled down against her feathers, while the male circled out in front, ready to lead the family off. Tom backed off and let them be. He watched through his binoculars as the little family moved across the bay, the chicks bobbing like corks, unseen by anyone who did not know to look.

That evening, lying in the tent before dark, he thought about the night before. The wailing he had heard was not loneliness at all, or not only that. It was a pair of birds keeping track of each other across a mile of black water, a family staying together in the dark. He fell asleep with the sound of them in his head, and when a loon called again at three he smiled into his pillow and did not get up.

## 28. 4 September — The Silent Hunter

*At the marsh hide. I have come here for two weeks and only tonight has the owl appeared. It was worth the wait.*

The hide smelled of damp reed and old wood. Ruth had been coming out here every clear evening for two weeks, sitting in the dark with her binoculars, waiting for the barn owls that lived in the derelict granary across the marsh. Twice she had heard them and seen nothing. Tonight, as the last of the light drained out of the sky, she heard the scrape of a call from the willow — dry, like a hinge being turned — and knew one was already awake.

She had read that a barn owl's hearing is so precise it can locate a mouse in total darkness from the sound of its footsteps alone. The heart-shaped disc of feathers around its face works like a funnel, directing sound to ears set at slightly different heights, so the bird can judge both distance and direction. It was this, she thought, not any magic of the night, that made the owl so lethal.

The first owl left the granary just as the sun went. It crossed the marsh low, pale as a torn scrap of paper in the last light — and it made no sound. Ruth had always found this the most astonishing thing: a bird a meter across, flying at walking pace, completely silent. The leading edges of its flight feathers are combed into stiff little fringes that break up the air before it can form turbulence, and the surfaces are velvety, soaking up the last of the noise. No rush of wings, no whistle. Only the faint clatter of a coot somewhere in the reeds, and the owl's own breathing, which she could not hear at all.

It flew with its head tilted, quartering the ground in a slow, deliberate pattern. Twice it dropped toward the grass and came up empty — a false alarm, perhaps a vole that had frozen too well. The third time it went down feet first, talons spread, and when it rose there was a field mouse hanging from one foot, already limp. The owl did not land. It swallowed the mouse whole, head first, with two quick movements of its neck, and continued across the marsh as if nothing had happened.

From the willow the scraping call came again, and this time the owl answered, turning toward the tree. Ruth saw it flare and drop onto a branch she could barely make out, a pale shape against the darker leaves. For a moment it sat perfectly still, then turned its heart-shaped face directly toward the hide. She had the absurd feeling that it was looking at her — really looking — measuring her the way it measured the grass for mice. Then it spread its wings and was gone, silent as ever, toward the granary and its mate.

Ruth sat on in the dark long after the owls had gone quiet. She thought about the pellets she had found beneath the granary the week before — grey and compressed, full of the fur and tiny bones of voles, the indigestible parts cast up after a meal. Everything about the bird was economical, precise: the silent wings, the funnel-shaped face, the swift swallow, the compact pellet. Nothing wasted.

When she finally unzipped the hide and stood up, the marsh was fully dark, full of the small sounds she had stopped noticing — the splash of a fish, the rustle of a rat in the reeds, the wind in the willow. She walked back to her car slowly, stepping carefully, listening. For the first time that evening she understood that the owl had not been hunting in spite of the darkness, but because of it: this was the hour when the marsh gave up its secrets, and only a bird built for silence could collect them.

## 29. 11 September — The Crossing

*At sea, three hundred miles off the American coast. A small bird rested on the rail for twenty minutes and then flew south into the dark. I shall think of it all winter.*

The ship was three hundred miles off the coast, halfway to Bermuda, when the deckhand found the bird on the rail at two in the morning. It was a warbler — small, olive-grey, with a pale eyebrow and a streaked breast — and it sat with its eyes half closed, breathing hard, its feathers fluffed against the wind. He had seen birds land on the ship before, during the migrations, and he knew better than to pick it up. He crouched beside it and shone his torch low so as not to dazzle it. The bird's eyes opened, bright and black, and for a moment it looked at him without fear.

It was a blackpoll warbler, though the deckhand did not know it. He did not know that this particular bird had left the coast of New England two nights before and was making a nonstop flight of nearly two thousand miles over the western Atlantic to the Caribbean — a journey that could take eighty hours or more, through day and night and whatever weather the ocean supplied. He did not know that birds of this species doubled their body weight before departure, packing on fat for the crossing, or that they navigated by the stars and by the earth's magnetic field, or that a storm could blow them hundreds of miles off course. He only knew that it was small, and tired, and very far from land.

He went below and brought back a dish of water and a few crumbs of biscuit, but the bird did not eat. It sat on the rail for perhaps twenty minutes while the ship rolled beneath it, sometimes closing its eyes, sometimes lifting its head to gaze south into the dark. Then it opened its wings, stretched them once, and stepped off the rail. The deckhand watched the torch beam catch it for a second — small, determined, flying low over the black water — and then it was gone into the night. He thought of it often during the rest of the voyage, somewhere out there in the dark, still going south.

## 30. 18 September — The Banding Station

*On a barrier island, in the netting lines before nine. The birds arrive in the night and are caught at dawn — a brief, harmless interruption of a journey of thousands of miles.*

The barrier island was a narrow strip of sand and scrub, and on an autumn morning a team of researchers had strung mist nets between the shrubs at its northern end. The nets were almost invisible — fine black mesh, set at chest height — and the birds, flying low through the scrub after a night's migration, flew straight into them. A woman named Sarah worked the nets, checking them every twenty minutes, and by nine o'clock she had a dozen birds in soft cloth bags: warblers, a vireo, a thrush, a small flycatcher.

She took each bird to the banding table and worked quickly. She identified the species, weighed it, measured its wing, checked its fat reserves by looking at the fat pad at the base of the belly, and slipped a small aluminum band onto its leg — a numbered ring that would tell the world, if the bird were ever caught again or found dead, where and when it had been. She had done this for eight autumns. Most of the birds were heading south, refueling on the island's berries and insects before the next leg over the water. One was a blackpoll warbler, small and very fat — a bird that would cross the ocean nonstop — and she handled it with particular care, noting the weight in her notebook before she released it.

The release was the best part. She walked a few paces into the scrub, opened her hand, and the bird did not fly at once but sat for a second, apparently confused, then shot off through the branches, gone. Some flew south immediately; others vanished into the cover to feed. Sarah wrote the band number in her log, and went back to check the nets. The migrated birds would never know they had been handled, except for the light ring on one leg — a small, permanent record of a morning on a barrier island, halfway between two continents.

## 31. 27 September — The Waiting Line

*Low tide on the estuary, and the flats are covered. I have never seen so many birds in one place, or heard such a continuous sound.*

At low tide the estuary was a vast, glistening plain of mud, and it was covered in birds. A woman on the seawall with a telescope watched the flock spread out across the exposed surface — thousands of waders probing for worms and small shellfish: red knots, dunlins, bar-tailed godwits, oystercatchers with their bright orange bills. It was September, and they were on their way south, stopping to refuel on the mudflats that stretched for miles along the coast. Each bird worked steadily, its bill going into the mud again and again, and the whole flat seemed to shimmer with their movement.

She had read that some of these birds would fly thousands of miles without stopping, and that a red knot could double its weight feeding on a mudflat like this before it set off. A godwit, she knew, might cross the Pacific in one flight. The flock fed for three hours as the tide began to turn. Then, as the water crept back over the mud, the birds gathered into dense lines at the water's edge, standing shoulder to shoulder, waiting for a place to roost at high tide. They looked like a dark, living belt drawn along the shoreline.

A peregrine falcon passed over the flats, and the reaction was instantaneous: the whole belt of birds exploded into the air, wheeling in a great swirling cloud above the water, thousands of wings turning together, then dividing and reforming. The falcon stooped once, missed, and flew off out to sea. The flock settled back onto the mud, and the woman lowered her telescope. The tide was still rising; the birds would wait there until it turned again, then feed once more through the night.

## 32. 5 October — The Broken Branch

*Australia in spring. I walked past this bird twice before I saw it — and even then I had to look through the lens to believe it.*

In the Australian spring a tawny frogmouth sat on a fence post at the edge of the paddock, and it looked exactly like a broken branch. It was a large bird, grey-brown and heavily mottled, with a broad head, a short hooked bill, and eyes that were small and half-closed. When a photographer, walking the track at dusk, stopped to examine the shape with his telephoto lens, the bird did not move. It simply sat with its head tilted slightly upward, staring past him, so perfectly camouflaged that he did not believe it was a bird until he saw the faint movement of its breast.

Frogmouths did not perch upright like other birds, he knew; they sat along a branch rather than across it, aligned with the wood, relying on their plumage and their stillness to disappear. This one was presumably hunting, though it looked asleep. He had read that they caught moths, beetles, and other insects at night — sometimes small mice and frogs — by sitting motionless and waiting, then making a sudden lunge with their huge, wide gape. The mouth, when it opened, was enormous, almost the width of the head. The bird gave no sign of it now.

He took a photograph and stepped closer. Still the bird did not move. Then, off in the trees, another frogmouth called — a low, resonant, hollow *oom-oom-oom*, like someone blowing across the top of a bottle — and the bird on the post slowly turned its head toward the sound. Its great mouth opened in a wide, silent yawn, and closed again. The photographer backed away. He had read that pairs called to each other before breeding, and that the same bird might return to the same post night after night. He walked back to his car in the dusk, and looked once more through the lens: the post appeared empty, the broken branch just a broken branch again.

## 33. 14 October — The Blue Collection

*Bowerbirds are the closest thing birds have to artists. This one had a collection of blue objects and painted his walls with a brush of grass.*

The bower stood in a clearing of the rainforest like a small, deliberate hut — a tunnel of sticks woven together, with a platform in front — and it was decorated with blue. A woman named Louise found it in October while walking a track in northern Queensland, and she stopped in amazement. Scattered around the entrance were bottle caps, clothespins, pieces of blue glass, a blue feather, several shiny blue berries, and a child's plastic toy, all arranged with care. The walls of the bower were stained with a dark paste, painted on with a wad of grass that the bird held in its bill like a brush; the paste was made of chewed plant material and saliva.

The owner, a male satin bowerbird, was glossy blue-black and about the size of a magpie. He watched her from a nearby branch, then flew down and began to tidy: picking up a blue object in his bill, turning it, placing it precisely, stepping back to assess the effect. She had read that bowerbirds did not attract females by song or by the beauty of their own plumage alone, but by the quality of the bower and the collection — that a male with the best display won the most mates, and that the females inspected bowers critically before choosing. The male's whole life in the breeding season seemed to consist of this: building, decorating, painting, rearranging, and displaying.

A female appeared — mottled greenish-brown, quite plain beside him — and the male went into his display at once. He crouched, spread his wings, lowered his head, and gave a loud, harsh call, while holding a blue object in his bill. The female walked slowly through the bower, inspecting, then turned and walked away into the trees. The male followed for a few paces, calling, then gave up and returned to his bower, where he picked up the clothespin and moved it two inches to the left. Louise backed quietly out of the clearing, and left him to his work.

## 34. 22 October — The Planter

*An oak wood in autumn, and a jay burying acorns. I stood and watched one bird do this twenty times in twenty minutes.*

In autumn an Eurasian jay carried an acorn across the oak wood, and a woman walking the path watched it land on a patch of bare earth, push the acorn into the leaf litter with its bill, and cover it over. Then it flew off to fetch another. She had read that a single jay could cache thousands of acorns in a season, remembering the location of each one, and that the ones it forgot or failed to retrieve grew into oak seedlings — which made the jay, in effect, the planter of the woodland. She watched it work for twenty minutes: an acorn fetched from a tree, carried to a hiding place, buried, and then another. A squirrel appeared and began digging nearby, and the jay gave a sharp, harsh call — a surprisingly convincing imitation of a buzzard — which sent the squirrel scrambling up a trunk. The jay waited until it had gone, then uncovered its acorn and reburied it elsewhere. She walked on, and thought of the oaks that might grow there in fifty years, planted by a bird that would never see them.

## 35. 3 November — The Two-Note Call

*The desert, after dark, with a recorder in a spinifex bush. Two notes, repeated four times. I have never forgotten the moment I heard them.*

The biologist sat in the dark of the Australian interior with an acoustic recorder hanging from a spinifex bush, listening to the night through headphones. For three weeks she had been setting recorders across a vast, stony desert, looking for a bird that almost no one had ever seen: the night parrot, a small, cryptic, ground-dwelling parrot that was nocturnal, nested in spinifex, and had been thought extinct for most of the twentieth century. Its call was distinctive — a short, high, two-note whistle, sometimes described as a muted "toop-toop" — and it was usually heard only in the hour after sunset.

At nine o'clock, through the static and the chirping of insects, she heard it: two clear notes, repeated four times, drifting from somewhere beyond the rise. She held her breath and turned up the gain. There it was again — the same two notes, unmistakable. She marked the time in her notebook, her hands shaking slightly. She did not see the bird that night; night parrots were famously difficult to see, even when calling. But the next morning she walked toward the rise, and in a patch of spinifex found a faint trail of trampled grass and a few feathers — greenish-yellow, with a dark patch — that she carefully collected into a paper envelope. She sat back on her heels in the heat. The call on the recording was proof enough that the bird was there, living its secret life in the desert night, and she had heard it with her own ears.

## 36. 18 December — The Rank Beach

*South Georgia, in the southern summer. Tens of thousands of king penguins on a black beach, and a noise that carries across the bay. The year ends as it began — with birds, and more birds than I can count.*

The beach on South Georgia was black sand and the noise hit them before they reached the water: a deafening, many-voiced braying that carried across the bay. It was December, high summer in the southern hemisphere, and the king penguin colony stretched for a mile along the shore, tens of thousands of birds packed so closely that the beach seemed to be paved in black and white and orange. The adults stood erect, tuxedoed, with vivid orange ear-patches; the chicks were brown and furry, like oversized stuffed toys, and many of them were already nearly as tall as their parents.

A small group of visitors picked its way along the designated path, trying not to step on the birds, which did not seem to care. One adult came ashore from the sea, dripping, and began calling — a loud, brassy bray — and a chick somewhere in the crowd answered. The parent pushed through the packed bodies, calling again, until it reached its own chick and fed it by regurgitation, the chick's head deep in the adult's bill. Nearby, an unguarded egg lay on the sand, and a skua — a brown, predatory gull — was watching it with obvious interest. A penguin turned, saw the skua, and lunged; the skua stepped back, but did not go far.

One penguin, fat and contented, lay on its belly and propelled itself up the beach with its feet, tobogganing through the crowd. It was the only one moving fast. The visitors stopped to watch, and then moved on, following the path. Behind them the colony continued its endless business — calling, feeding, guarding, preening — a city of birds with no room left on the beach, spilling up the slopes toward the tussock grass.

# AFTERWORD

The year is done. The notebook is full, and the pages near the end are written in a different hand than the pages near the beginning — hurried, less careful, as if I too sensed the year running out.

I have not tried to draw conclusions. If there is a pattern in these thirty-six entries, it is not one I put there deliberately. It is simply the pattern of a year: the visitors of winter, the clamor of spring, the heat and ferocity of summer, the long movements of autumn, and the far south in December, where the birds outnumber everything.

There is one thread I noticed only when I came to read the entries back. In January, in a flooded field, I watched geese turn the landscape into an illusion of snow. In February, on a dune, I watched a snowy owl that looked like a sculpture left out in the weather. In April, on a country road, two red sparks in the headlights turned out to be a bird. In December, on a beach at the end of the world, I could not count the penguins. Again and again I found myself looking at a bird and failing, for a moment, to see a bird at all — seeing instead a broken branch, a piece of the road, a snowdrift, a statue.

That, I think, is what a year of watching does to a person. It makes the world strange again. I would not have it any other way.

— *E. V., December, with the last page turned*
